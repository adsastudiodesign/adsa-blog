The Landlord's Rent Roll: How to Track Rent Payments in a Spreadsheet

Ask a landlord with three or more units how rent collection is going, and you'll often get a vague answer. Not because they're disorganized people — because rent tracking usually lives in bank statements, text messages, and memory. That works until a tenant pays partial, pays late, or disputes what they paid in March.

A rent roll fixes this. It's a simple ledger — one row per unit per month — showing what was owed, what was paid, when, and what's outstanding. Here's how to set one up so it actually gets used.

What a rent roll tracks

At minimum, each row needs:

  • Property and unit — which door this payment belongs to
  • Tenant name — who's on the lease
  • Month — the period the payment covers
  • Rent due — the contracted monthly amount
  • Amount paid — what actually arrived
  • Date paid — when it arrived
  • Balance — due minus paid, calculated automatically
  • Status — paid, partial, late, or overdue

The status column is the whole point. A quick filter for "overdue" should answer "who owes me money right now?" in five seconds. If it takes longer than that, the system isn't working.

Late fees: decide the policy once, then automate it

Nothing poisons a landlord-tenant relationship like improvised late fees. Decide your policy up front — a flat fee, a daily rate after a grace period, whatever your lease and local laws allow — and bake it into the sheet as a formula.

For example: if the grace period is 5 days and the fee is $50, the late-fee column can calculate automatically from the date paid. The tenant sees a consistent, lease-backed number every time instead of a judgment call. Consistency is what makes late fees enforceable and relationships survivable.

(Check your state and local laws on late fee limits — they vary, and leases must reflect what's legally allowed.)

Partial payments need their own handling

Partial payments are where informal tracking breaks down. A tenant sends $800 of $1,200 rent with a note saying "rest Friday." Two weeks later, nobody remembers whether Friday happened.

Your rent roll should show partial payments explicitly: amount paid, remaining balance, and a follow-up date. The balance column carries the remainder forward so it can't silently vanish. When Friday comes, the outstanding balance is sitting there waiting — not reconstructed from text messages.

Reconcile monthly, not yearly

Set a recurring 15-minute appointment: the first weekend of each month, open the rent roll and confirm every unit. What's the point of tracking if the data goes stale? Monthly reconciliation catches:

  • Payments that arrived but weren't logged
  • Tenants who drifted into "I'll catch up next month" territory
  • Lease renewals coming due in the next 60–90 days

That last one matters more than most landlords think. An expired lease that rolls to month-to-month without a rent review is money left on the table — or a tenant you meant to have a conversation with, six months ago.

Connect the rent roll to the bigger picture

A rent roll tells you who paid. But a landlord also needs to know: is each property actually profitable? That's income (rent roll) minus expenses (maintenance, insurance, taxes, mortgage, vacancies), per property, per year.

Keep an expense log with the same discipline — date, property, category, amount, receipt link or note. At year-end, each property gets a simple profit-and-loss: total rent collected minus total expenses. This is also what your tax preparer wants, and producing it in January instead of reconstructing it in April is worth real money in reduced accountant hours.

What about property management software?

Software like AppFolio or Buildium is excellent — for portfolios large enough to justify the monthly cost. For landlords with 1–10 units, a well-built spreadsheet does everything the software does at the rent-tracking level, with no subscription and no learning curve beyond Excel or Google Sheets.

The tradeoff is honest: software sends automatic reminders and tenant portals; a spreadsheet requires your 15 minutes a month. For a small portfolio, that's a good trade.

Document everything at move-in and move-out

The rent roll tracks money; a condition log protects it. Before a tenant moves in, walk the unit together and photograph everything — walls, floors, appliances, fixtures — with timestamps. Store the photo links alongside the tenant record. Do the same at move-out.

This isn't about distrust. It's about the security deposit conversation going smoothly because both sides are looking at the same dated evidence instead of competing memories. Most deposit disputes that reach small claims court are decided by documentation, and the landlord with photos wins far more often than the landlord with opinions.

Add two fields to your tenant record: move-in condition link and move-out condition link. Fill them the day of the walkthrough, not "later."

Separate repairs from capital improvements

Not every maintenance dollar is the same. A $150 faucet repair and a $4,000 HVAC replacement hit your books — and your taxes — differently. Tag each maintenance entry as repair (keeps the property operating) or capital improvement (extends life or adds value).

Why it matters: repairs are generally expensed in the year paid; improvements are typically depreciated over years. Your expense log should carry this distinction from day one, because reconstructing it in April from a pile of receipts is where landlords overpay their accountants — or misreport. When in doubt, your tax preparer decides the classification; your job is giving them clean, tagged data.

Screen like your cash flow depends on it (it does)

The cheapest maintenance is a tenant who pays on time and reports issues early. A consistent screening process — application, credit/background check where legal, income verification (typically 3× rent), rental history, and actually calling previous landlords — prevents most of the horror stories.

Keep a simple applicant log: name, property applied for, date, screening outcome, reason if declined. Consistent written criteria protect you legally (fair housing compliance) and practically (no "I liked them" exceptions that become eviction proceedings).
