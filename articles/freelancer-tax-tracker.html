Freelancer Taxes Without the Panic: How to Set Aside the Right Amount Every Month

Every freelancer knows the feeling: it's April, the tax bill arrives, and the money you thought was "yours" turns out to have belonged to the IRS all along. The problem isn't that freelancer taxes are complicated. It's that no one withholds them for you — so the money sits in your account looking spendable until it suddenly isn't.

The fix is a system, not willpower. Here's how to set aside the right amount every month so tax season becomes a non-event.

Understand what you actually owe

As a freelancer in the US, you generally owe two layers of tax on your profit (income minus deductible expenses):

  1. Self-employment tax — 15.3% on your net earnings (Social Security + Medicare). This surprises new freelancers the most because employees only see half of it; your employer used to pay the other half.
  2. Income tax — your regular federal (and state, if applicable) income tax on top.

A common rule of thumb is to set aside 25–30% of profit. That's not your exact rate — your real rate depends on total income, deductions, and state — but it's a safe holding zone while you learn your numbers. (This is general information, not tax advice; a tax professional can pin down your actual rate.)

The key word is *profit*, not revenue. If you earned $6,000 and spent $1,500 on deductible business expenses, you set aside 25–30% of $4,500, not $6,000. Tracking expenses isn't just good hygiene — it directly shrinks the amount you need to hold back.

The separate-account method

The simplest system that works:

  1. Open a second checking or savings account — a "tax holding" account.
  2. Every time client money lands, move 25–30% of the *profit portion* into the holding account immediately. Same day. Before you spend anything.
  3. Pay your quarterly estimated taxes out of the holding account.

"Immediately" is doing all the work here. Money that never sits in your spending account never gets mentally spent. Freelancers who "plan to set it aside at month-end" consistently come up short; freelancers who move it on receipt consistently don't.

Know the quarterly deadlines

The IRS expects estimated tax payments four times a year. For 2026, the approximate deadlines are:

  • April 15 (for Jan–Mar income)
  • June 15 (for Apr–May income)
  • September 15 (for Jun–Aug income)
  • January 15 (of the next year, for Sep–Dec income)

Miss them and you can owe underpayment penalties — not huge, but an avoidable leak. Put all four dates in your calendar now, with a reminder a week before each.

Track it monthly in one place

You need three numbers, updated monthly:

  • Income received this month (per client is better — you'll want it at tax time)
  • Deductible expenses this month (with categories: software, home office, mileage, supplies, etc.)
  • Tax set aside this month (what you actually moved to the holding account)

Then compare "set aside" against your target (25–30% of profit). If there's a gap, you know *this month* — not next April.

Mileage deserves special mention: if you drive for work, log every trip (date, destination, business purpose, miles). At the standard mileage rate, this is often a freelancer's largest single deduction, and it's the one most people reconstruct badly from memory in April.

Invoices are part of the tax system

Unpaid invoices distort everything. If $4,000 of your "income" is sitting in overdue invoices, your profit number is fiction and your set-aside math is wrong. Track every invoice with its status — sent, paid, overdue — and follow up on overdue ones weekly. Getting paid on time isn't just cash flow; it's what makes your tax numbers real.

What to do with the surplus

Some years you'll over-save (good problem). When you file and the actual bill is less than your holding account, you have options: roll it into next year's first quarterly payment, move it to an emergency fund, or invest it. The point is that the decision happens from a position of surplus, not scramble.

Separate business money from personal money

This deserves its own section because it underpins everything above: run your freelance income through a dedicated business account. Not because the IRS requires it (it doesn't, for sole proprietors), but because mixing makes every number fuzzy.

With a separate account, "profit" is simply what the business account holds after expenses — no untangling grocery runs from software subscriptions. Come tax time, your income log and your bank statements tell the same story, which is exactly what you want if you're ever questioned. It also makes the separate tax-holding account natural: business account → tax holding account, two transfers, done.

Know your common deductions

You don't need to memorize the tax code, but knowing the big categories changes behavior — you start keeping receipts for things you used to ignore:

  • Home office — if you have a dedicated workspace, a portion of rent/mortgage, utilities, and internet may be deductible (simplified or actual-expense method).
  • Software and subscriptions — design tools, hosting, project management apps, stock assets.
  • Health insurance premiums — self-employed filers can often deduct these (with conditions).
  • Retirement contributions — SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income *and* build your future; they also lower the profit your set-aside percentage applies to.
  • Education and training — courses and books that maintain or improve your current professional skills.
  • Mileage and travel — as noted above, log it contemporaneously or lose it.

The pattern: if it's ordinary and necessary for your work, it probably counts. When in doubt, track it and ask a professional at filing time — untracked expenses are deductions you can never claim.
